Fluid flow in industrial and natural systems is rarely purely hydrodynamic. In applications 
like induction melting, casting, electromagnetic stirring, and fusion blanket design, applied 
magnetic fields are deliberately used to control flow structure. The canonical model for 
boundary-driven flow is the lid-driven cavity: a fluid-filled box set in motion by a moving 
wall. It is well-studied, well-benchmarked (Ghia and Shatrov citation), and clean enough that 
the effect of adding a magnetic field is easy to isolate.

When a magnetic field is introduced, the Lorentz force are generated in a conducting fluid which 
act as body forces. The generated forces compete with inertia. Vortices that persist in the purely 
hydrodynamic case get suppressed, boundary layers form along field lines, and the flow topology changes 
qualitatively depending on field strength and orientation. The interaction parameter N and the Reynolds 
number Re together define where in this regime map a given flow lives.

In the problem of interest, we focus on the square lid-driven cavity with an electrically conducting
liquid. The main parameters of interest as mentioned in the earlier paragraph are: Reynolds number, 
$Re = \frac{\rho UL}{\mu}$, that governs the ratio of inertial to viscous forces in the fluid, and 
the Interaction parameter, $N = \frac{\sigma B^2 L}{\rho U}$, that governs the strength of magnetic 
field to the inertial forces in the fluid. We operate in the low magnetic Reynolds number regime, 
$R_m = \mu_0 \sigma UL ≪ 1$, where the induced magnetic field is negligible compared to the applied 
field. This is the relevant limit for liquid metals at industrial scales and allows 
the applied field B to be treated as prescribed and frozen.

## Physics 

The equations of interest for magnetic field are the electric potential equation (1) (derived from the Maxwell's equations),
and Ohm's law (2) that determined the current density in the domain for a given potential gradient and applied magnetic field.

$$\nabla^2 \phi = \nabla \cdot (\mathbf{u} \times \mathbf{B}) \tag{1}$$

$$\mathbf{j} = -\nabla \phi + \mathbf{u} \times \mathbf{B} \tag{2}$$

The hydrodynamic equations are taken from the Navier-Stokes, which solve a conservation of momentum (3) and a conservation
of mass (4), for which incompressibility condition is assumed.

$$\rho \left( \frac{\partial \mathbf{u}}{\partial t} + \mathbf{u} \cdot \nabla \mathbf{u} \right) = -\nabla p + \mu \nabla^2 \mathbf{u} + \mathbf{F}_L \tag{3}$$

$$\nabla \cdot \mathbf{u} = 0 \tag{4}$$.

The body force in the momentum equation (3) is computed as the cross product between current density and the magnetic field in the 
fluid (5). 

$$ \mathbf{F}_L = \mathbf{j} \times \mathbf{B} \tag{5}$$


## Discretization

A staggered grid is used for velocity, pressure, and potential. The x-component 
of velocity lives on u-faces, the y-component on v-faces, and pressure and potential 
sit at cell centers. Ghost cells surround the domain on all sides to enforce boundary 
conditions without special-casing the interior stencils.

| Field | Location | Shape |
|---|---|---|
| p, φ | cell centers + ghosts | (N+2, N+2) |
| u, Bx, jx | x-faces | (N+2, N+1) |
| v, By, jy | y-faces | (N+1, N+2) |
| Bz, jz, ωz | corners | (N+1, N+1) |

This arrangement avoids the pressure-velocity decoupling (checkerboarding) that occurs 
on a collocated grid, where pressure and velocity sample the same points and a spurious 
oscillatory pressure field can satisfy the discrete continuity equation undetected.

Gradients, divergences, and the Laplacian are each written as standalone functions 
operating directly on these staggered fields, with interpolation operators (`_u_to_c`, 
`_v_to_u`, `_n_to_u`, and their counterparts) handling the transfers between lattices 
needed for the convective and Lorentz force terms.

## Implementation in JAX

The solver is built around a small set of operators acting on staggered fields: 
`grad`, `div`, `laplacian`, and `cross`. Momentum, the pressure projection, and the 
Lorentz force are all assembled from these same functions rather than hand-written 
per term. Adding a new transported quantity, a temperature field for buoyancy, say, 
is a matter of writing its governing equation with these operators and wiring it 
into the time step, not rewriting the discretization.

```python
def grad(p, params):
    N, dx, dy = params.N, params.dx, params.dy
    return ((p[:, 1:N+2] - p[:, :N+1])  / dx,
            (p[:N+1, :]  - p[1:N+2, :]) / dy)

def div(u, v, params):
    N, dx, dy = params.N, params.dx, params.dy
    return ((u[1:N+1, 1:N+1] - u[1:N+1, :N])    / dx +
            (v[:N,   1:N+1]  - v[1:N+1, 1:N+1]) / dy)

def laplacian(u, v, params):
    N, dx, dy = params.N, params.dx, params.dy
    lap_x = ((u[1:N+1, 2:N+1] - 2*u[1:N+1, 1:N] + u[1:N+1, 0:N-1]) / dx**2
           + (u[0:N,   1:N]   - 2*u[1:N+1, 1:N] + u[2:N+2, 1:N])   / dy**2)
    lap_y = ((v[1:N,   2:N+2] - 2*v[1:N, 1:N+1] + v[1:N,   0:N])   / dx**2
           + (v[0:N-1, 1:N+1] - 2*v[1:N, 1:N+1] + v[2:N+1, 1:N+1]) / dy**2)
    return lap_x, lap_y
```

`cross` is the one operator that does real work beyond finite differencing, since 
the three components of a cross product live on three different lattices (faces, 
faces, corners) and each output component needs its inputs interpolated onto its 
own lattice before the product is taken:

```python
def cross(ax, ay, az, bx, by, bz, N):
    out_x = _v_to_u(ay, N)*_n_to_u(bz, N) - _n_to_u(az, N)*_v_to_u(by, N)
    out_y = _n_to_v(az, N)*_u_to_v(bx, N) - _u_to_v(ax, N)*_n_to_v(bz, N)
    out_z = _u_to_n(ax, N)*_v_to_n(by, N) - _v_to_n(ay, N)*_u_to_n(bx, N)
    return out_x, out_y, out_z
```

This single function computes both `u × B` for the electric potential source and 
`j × B` for the Lorentz force, just with different field arguments.

### Time stepping and the pressure solve

Explicit time integration is used throughout. The scheme is RK2 Heun, a 
predictor-corrector method that achieves second-order accuracy in time with 
two evaluations of the right-hand side per step.

The idea is simple: take a full Euler step to get a predicted state, evaluate 
the RHS there, then average the two slopes to get a better estimate of the 
true trajectory over the interval.

$$u^* = u^n + \Delta t \, H(u^n)$$
$$u^{n+1} = \frac{1}{2}\left(u^n + u^*\right) + \frac{1}{2} \Delta t \, H(u^*)$$

A pressure projection enforces incompressibility after each stage. The time 
step is constrained by the convective CFL condition, $\Delta t < \Delta x / u_{max}$, 
with the lid velocity used as a floor on $u_{max}$ to avoid an over-optimistic 
estimate at early times when the flow is still developing.

The implementation of the time-stepping scheme in JAX is as follows: 

```python
def step(state: State, params: Params) -> State:
    N, dt = params.N, params.dt
    u0, v0 = state.u, state.v

    H_x, H_y, state = compute_H(state, params, print_debug=False)
    state = state._replace(
        u=u0.at[1:N+1, 1:N  ].set(u0[1:N+1, 1:N]   + dt * H_x),
        v=v0.at[1:N,   1:N+1].set(v0[1:N,   1:N+1] + dt * H_y),
    )
    state = apply_bcs(state, params)
    state = project(state, params)
    state = apply_bcs(state, params)

    H_x, H_y, state = compute_H(state, params, print_debug=False)
    state = state._replace(
        u=u0.at[1:N+1, 1:N  ].set(0.5*(u0[1:N+1, 1:N]   + state.u[1:N+1, 1:N])   + 0.5*dt*H_x),
        v=v0.at[1:N,   1:N+1].set(0.5*(v0[1:N,   1:N+1] + state.v[1:N,   1:N+1]) + 0.5*dt*H_y),
    )
    state = apply_bcs(state, params)
    state = project(state, params)
    state = apply_bcs(state, params)

    return state._replace(t=state.t + dt, step_count=state.step_count + 1)
```

### Poisson solves: spectral method

Two Poisson equations appear per time step, one for pressure in the projection 
step and one for the electric potential $\phi$. Both are solved spectrally [[3]](#references).

On a uniform grid with Neumann boundary conditions, the eigenfunctions of the 
discrete Laplacian are known in closed form: they are the modes of the discrete 
cosine transform (DCT-II). This means the Poisson equation diagonalizes exactly 
under a DCT, turning a linear system into a pointwise division in frequency space:

$$\hat{p}_{ij} = \frac{\hat{f}_{ij}}{\lambda_i + \lambda_j}, \qquad 
\lambda_k = \frac{2(\cos(\pi k / N) - 1)}{\Delta x^2}$$

where $\hat{\cdot}$ denotes the DCT coefficient and $\lambda_k$ are the 
eigenvalues of the discrete Laplacian. The solution is recovered by an inverse 
DCT. The full solve is one forward transform, one division, one inverse transform, 
with no iteration, no convergence tolerance, and no matrix to store. On an 
$N \times N$ grid this is $\mathcal{O}(N^2 \log N)$, the same complexity as 
a single FFT. In practice this reduced solve time from minutes per call with 
SOR to milliseconds, making the 800-run parameter sweep feasible on a laptop.

The Poisson solves inside `project` (and inside the electric potential equation) 
use a spectral method.

```python
def _poisson_solve_spectral(field, source_interior, N, dx, remove_mean=True):
    
    src = source_interior - jnp.mean(source_interior) if remove_mean else source_interior
    
    i = jnp.arange(N)
    lam = 2 * (jnp.cos(jnp.pi * i / N) - 1) / dx**2
    LAM = lam[:, None] + lam[None, :]
    
    src_hat = jax.scipy.fft.dctn(src, type=2, norm='ortho')  # or jax version
    sol_hat = src_hat / LAM
    sol_hat = sol_hat.at[0, 0].set(0.0)
    sol = jax.scipy.fft.idctn(sol_hat, type=2, norm='ortho')
    
    p_full = jnp.zeros((N+2, N+2)).at[1:-1, 1:-1].set(sol)
    return _apply_neumann(p_full, N)
```

This is a direct solve, one forward transform, one division, one inverse transform, 
with no convergence tolerance or iteration count to tune.

## Validation

The solver is validated against Shatrov et al. [[1]](#references), who report 
streamline structure for the MHD lid-driven cavity at Re = 5000 with B = B₀·e_x 
across a range of interaction parameters N. Because Bz = 0 for this field orientation, 
the electric potential vanishes identically and the Lorentz force reduces to algebraic 
braking on the cross-field velocity component, the Hartmann configuration described 
in Section 1.

Four cases are compared: N = 0.0 (no field, pure hydrodynamic baseline), and 
N = 0.1, 0.3, 0.4.

At N = 0.0 the flow shows the expected multi-vortex structure for Re = 5000: a 
primary vortex shifted toward the downstream corner, with two secondary corner 
vortices and the beginning of tertiary structure, consistent with Ghia et al. [[2]](#references).

As N increases, the Lorentz force progressively suppresses the secondary vortices 
and elongates the primary vortex along the field direction. By N = 0.4 the flow 
matches the topology reported in Shatrov's Figure 4: a single dominant vortex 
stretched horizontally, with the lower secondary vortex compressed into a thin 
recirculation near the bottom wall. The match is qualitative rather than pointwise, 
the reference figure is read off a published plot rather than tabulated data, but 
the vortex count, relative sizes, and elongation direction agree closely.

The settling behavior shown above is consistent with this picture: higher N 
accelerates convergence to steady state, since the Lorentz force adds dissipation 
that the viscous term alone does not provide at Re = 5000. The N = 0.0 case at this 
Reynolds number is still slowly decaying at t = 50, which becomes relevant later 
when generating a dataset across the full (Re, N) parameter space, the unsettled 
high-Re, low-N corner of that space captures the approach to transition rather than 
a true steady state.

## Closing

The results confirm that the inductionless MHD cavity is a clean testbed for 
studying the competition between inertia and magnetic braking. Even at moderate 
interaction parameter N = 0.4, the flow topology changes qualitatively, which 
makes the (Re, N) parameter space interesting rather than just a smooth 
interpolation between two known limits.

The immediate next step is surrogate modeling. An 800-run dataset spanning 
Re ∈ [100, 5000] and N ∈ [0, 1] for two field orientations is underway, generated 
using the spectral solver described above. The goal is a Fourier Neural Operator [[6]](#references)
that learns the map from (Re, N, B) to the steady-state flow field, and eventually 
to use that surrogate inside a data assimilation loop: given sparse velocity 
observations, reconstruct the full field and infer the governing parameters. The cavity
is simple enough to be a clean testbed for this pipeline before applying on complex industrial
settings

On the solver side, the natural extensions are a non-uniform grid for better 
Hartmann layer resolution at high Ha, and a time-varying B for rotating or 
travelling magnetic field configurations relevant to electromagnetic stirring.

Code, validation notebook, and dataset generation scripts are at 
[github.com/maru2000/cavity_mhd](https://github.com/maru2000/cavity_mhd). 
The next post will cover the dataset, the FNO architecture, and what the operator 
learns about the regime map.
