Fluid flow in industrial and natural systems is rarely purely hydrodynamic. In applications 
like induction melting, casting, electromagnetic stirring, and fusion blanket design, applied 
magnetic fields are deliberately used to control flow structure. The canonical model for 
boundary-driven flow is the lid-driven cavity: a fluid-filled box set in motion by a moving 
wall. It is well-studied, well-benchmarked, and clean enough that the effect of adding a 
magnetic field is easy to isolate.

When a magnetic field is introduced, the Lorentz force competes with inertia. Vortices that 
persist in the purely hydrodynamic case get suppressed, boundary layers form along field lines, 
and the flow topology changes qualitatively depending on field strength and orientation. The 
interaction parameter N and the Reynolds number Re together define where in this regime map 
a given flow lives.

In the problem of interest, we focus on the square lid-driven cavity with an electrically conducting
liquid. The main parameters of interest as mentioned in the earlier paragraph are: Reynolds number, 
$Re = \frac{\rho UL}{\mu}$, that governs the ratio of intertial to viscous forces in the fluid, and 
the Interaction parameter, $N = \frac{\sigma B^2 L}{\rho U}$, that governs the strength of magnetic 
field to the inertial forces in the fluid. We operate in the low magnetic Reynolds number regime, 
$R_m = \mu_0 \sigma UL ≪ 1$, where the induced magnetic field is negligible compared to the applied 
field. This is the relevant limit for liquid metals at industrial scales and allows 
the applied field B to be treated as prescribed and frozen.

## Section 2: Discretization

## Section 2: Discretization

A staggered (MAC) grid is used for velocity, pressure, and potential. The x-component 
of velocity lives on u-faces, the y-component on v-faces, and pressure and potential 
sit at cell centers. Ghost cells surround the domain on all sides to enforce boundary 
conditions without special-casing the interior stencils.

| Field | Location | Shape |
|---|---|---|
| p, φ | cell centers + ghosts | (N+2, N+2) |
| u, Bx, jx | x-faces | (N+2, N+1) |
| v, By, jy | y-faces | (N+1, N+2) |
| Bz, jz, ωz | corners | (N+1, N+1) |

This arrangement avoids the pressure-velocity decoupling (checkerboarding) that occurs 
on a collocated grid, where pressure and velocity sample the same points and a spurious 
oscillatory pressure field can satisfy the discrete continuity equation undetected.

![Staggered MAC grid](/assets/images/staggered_grid.svg)

Gradients, divergences, and the Laplacian are each written as standalone functions 
operating directly on these staggered fields, with interpolation operators (`_u_to_c`, 
`_v_to_u`, `_n_to_u`, and their counterparts) handling the transfers between lattices 
needed for the convective and Lorentz force terms.
